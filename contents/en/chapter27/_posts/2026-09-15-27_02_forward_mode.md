Associate to every node $$v$$ a **tangent** $$\dot v = \frac{\partial v}{\partial t}$$ along a chosen input direction (one scalar seed). If $$v=h(u,w)$$ then

$$
\dot v = \partial_u h\cdot \dot u + \partial_w h\cdot \dot w.
$$

Running the original program *together with* these tangent recurrences yields a **Jacobian–vector product** $$J_f(x)\, \dot x$$ at essentially a small constant factor times the cost of $$f$$. That is **forward-mode AD**.

If $$f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$$ with $$n\ll m$$ (few inputs, many outputs), forward mode is cheap: $$n$$ seeded sweeps give the full Jacobian. If $$n\gg m$$ — one loss, a million weights — forward mode is the wrong default. You would need a million sweeps.

Finite differences $$[f(x+\varepsilon e_i)-f(x)]/\varepsilon$$ approximate the same Jacobian–vector products but waste precision and need a step $$\varepsilon$$. Forward AD is exact up to floating-point, with no $$\varepsilon$$.

**Tiny walk-through.** $$f(x_1,x_2)=x_1 x_2 + \sin(x_1)$$, seed $$\dot x=(1,0)$$. Then $$\dot f = x_2 + \cos(x_1)$$, which is $$\partial_{x_1}f$$. A second seed $$(0,1)$$ yields $$\partial_{x_2}f=x_1$$.

Forward mode is what you want for sensitivities of a *simulation* with few parameters (ODE shooting, some Hessian–vector recipes via “forward-over-reverse”). Deep learning almost always wants the next lesson.
