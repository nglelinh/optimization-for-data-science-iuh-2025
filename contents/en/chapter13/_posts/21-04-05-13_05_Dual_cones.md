When you first learned Lagrange duality, every inequality constraint $$h_i(x) \le 0$$ came with a nonnegative multiplier $$u_i \ge 0$$—geometrically, $$u$$ lived in the nonnegative orthant $$\mathbb{R}^m_+$$. What happens when your constraint is no longer a list of scalar inequalities, but a matrix semidefinite constraint $$X \succeq 0$$ or a second-order cone constraint $$\|x\|_2 \le t$$? You need a generalized notion of "nonnegative multiplier" that keeps inner products nonnegative against the cone—and that object is the **dual cone** $$K^*$$.

## Key ideas: Geometry and Algebra of Dual Cones

- **Pillar 1 — Acute-Angle Definition:** $$K^* = \{y : y^T x \ge 0\ \forall x \in K\}$$ is the set of all vectors forming an angle of at most $$90^\circ$$ with every ray in $$K$$. Because it is an intersection of closed halfspaces, $$K^*$$ is *always* a closed convex cone.
- **Pillar 2 — Self-Dual Cones Dominate Convex Optimization:** The three workhorse cones—nonnegative orthant $$\mathbb{R}^n_+$$, second-order (Lorentz) cone $$\mathcal{Q}^n$$, and positive semidefinite cone $$\mathbb{S}^n_+$$—all satisfy $$K^* = K$$.
- **Pillar 3 — Polarity & Norm-Cone Pairing:** Narrower primal cones produce wider dual cones (if $$K_1 \subseteq K_2$$, then $$K_2^* \subseteq K_1^*$$). For a norm cone $$K = \{(x,t) : \|x\| \le t\}$$, its dual is the dual-norm cone $$K^* = \{(y,s) : \|y\|_* \le s\}$$.
- **Pillar 4 — Conic Duality Made Automatic:** For $$\min_x f(x)$$ s.t. $$Ax \in K$$, the support function $$I_K^*(-u)$$ equals $$I_{K^*}(u)$$, immediately yielding the conic dual $$\max_{u \in K^*} -f^*(A^T u)$$.

### Worked example: Computing a 2D Dual Cone and Checking PSD Self-Duality

**Part A — A 2D polyhedral cone:** Let $$K = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x_1 \ge 0,\ x_2 \ge 2x_1\}$$ (a narrow wedge between the positive $$x_2$$-axis and the ray $$(1,2)^T$$).

1. **Identify the extreme rays of $$K$$:** Any $$x \in K$$ is a nonnegative combination of $$r_1 = (0, 1)^T$$ and $$r_2 = (1, 2)^T$$.
2. **Enforce $$y^T x \ge 0$$ on the generators:** $$y \in K^* \iff y^T r_1 \ge 0$$ and $$y^T r_2 \ge 0$$, which gives:
   $$y_2 \ge 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad y_1 + 2y_2 \ge 0 \iff y_2 \ge -\frac{1}{2}y_1.$$
   Notice that $$K$$ is a narrow wedge (aperture $$\approx 26.6^\circ$$), while $$K^*$$ is a wide obtuse wedge (aperture $$\approx 153.4^\circ$$), summing to $$180^\circ$$!

**Part B — Checking $$Y \in (\mathbb{S}^2_+)^*$$:** Take $$Y = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$ and $$X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \succeq 0$$.
3. **Compute the Frobenius inner product:** $$\operatorname{tr}(YX) = 2(1) + (-1)(1) + (-1)(1) + 2(1) = 2 \ge 0$$. Because $$Y$$ has eigenvalues $$1, 3 > 0$$, $$\operatorname{tr}(YX) = \sum_i \lambda_i(X)\, v_i^T Y v_i \ge 0$$ for *all* $$X \succeq 0$$, illustrating $$(\mathbb{S}^2_+)^* = \mathbb{S}^2_+$$.

## Dual cones
There exists a cone $$K ⊆ \mathbb{R}^n$$.
(Recalling the content covered earlier in [02-06-01]({% multilang_post_url contents/chapter02/21-02-11-02_06_01_Dual_cones %}), this means $$x \in K, t ≥ 0 \to tx \in K$$.)
> $$ K∗ = \{ y : y^Tx ≥ 0 \text{ for all } x \in K \} $$

This is called a **dual cone**, and it is always a convex cone (even when $$K$$ is not convex).

##### [Note]
$$y \in K∗ \iff \text{ the halfspace } \{ x : y^Tx ≥ 0 \} \text { contains } K$$
(From B & V page 52)

An important property here is that if $$K$$ is closed and a convex cone, then $$K^{∗∗} = K$$.

#### Examples:
• The dual cone of a linear subspace $$V$$ is $$V^{⊥}$$, i.e., the orthogonal complement.
E.g., $$(row(A))^{∗} = null(A) $$

• The dual cone of the norm cone $$K = \{ (x,t) \in \mathbb{R}^n+1 : \| x \|≤ t \}$$ is the norm cone of its dual norm $$K^{∗} = \{ (y,s) \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1} : \| y \|_{∗} ≤ s \}$$.

• The positive semidefinite cone $$\mathbb{S}^n_+$$ is a self-dual convex cone, meaning $$(\mathbb{S}^n_+)^{∗} = \mathbb{S}^n_+$$.

## Dual cones and dual problems
Consider the cone constrained problem:

> $$ \min_x f(x) \text{ subject to } Ax \in K$$

When $$I^{∗}_K(y) = \max_{z\in K} z^Ty$$ is the support function of $$K$$,
the dual problem of the above expression is as follows:
> $$ \max_u −f^∗(A^Tu)−I^∗_K(−u)$$


When $$K$$ is a cone, this can be easily defined as follows:
> $$ \max_u −f^∗(A^Tu) \text{ subject to } u \in K^{∗} $$

Here $$K^{∗}$$ is the dual cone of $$K$$. because $$= I_K^{*}(-u) \ I_{K^{*}}(−u) $$ 

This is quite useful because many different types of constraints can appear as cone constraints.
