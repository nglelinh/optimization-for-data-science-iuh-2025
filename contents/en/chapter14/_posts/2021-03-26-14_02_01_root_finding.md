Long before modern optimization existed, Isaac Newton and Joseph Raphson wanted to find where a curve crosses zero: $$F(x) = 0$$. Their trick was breathtakingly simple—replace the curved function $$F$$ by its straight tangent line at $$x$$ and walk to where that tangent hits zero. Applying that exact same tangent-line trick to the gradient $$F(x) = \nabla f(x) = 0$$ gives Newton's method for optimization!

## Key ideas: From Root Finding to Optimization

- **Pillar 1 — Linearization of $$F(x) = 0$$:** Approximating $$F(y) \approx F(x) + J_F(x)(y - x) = 0$$ yields the root-finding update $$x^+ = x - J_F(x)^{-1}F(x)$$.
- **Pillar 2 — Setting $$F = \nabla f$$ Recovers Optimization Newton:** When $$F(x) = \nabla f(x)$$, its Jacobian is the symmetric Hessian $$J_F(x) = \nabla^2 f(x)$$, turning root-finding into stationary-point finding.
- **Pillar 3 — Stationary Points vs. Minima:** Root-finding does not distinguish between minima, maxima, and saddle points; convexity ($$\nabla^2 f(x) \succ 0$$) is what guarantees the root of $$\nabla f(x) = 0$$ is a global minimum.
- **Pillar 4 — Nonlinear Systems in Interior-Point Methods:** Later in Chapters 15–17, primal-dual solvers apply root-finding Newton directly to the nonlinear KKT equations $$F(x, u, v) = 0$$.

### Worked example: Solving a 2D Nonlinear System with Root-Finding Newton

Find a root of $$F(x_1, x_2) = \begin{bmatrix} x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 5 \\ x_1 - x_2 - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$ (exact solution in the first quadrant: $$x^\star = (2, 1)^T$$), starting from $$x^{(0)} = (3, 1)^T$$.

1. **Compute the Jacobian matrix $$J_F(x)$$:**
   $$J_F(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 2x_1 & 2x_2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}.$$
2. **Evaluate $$F$$ and $$J_F$$ at $$x^{(0)} = (3, 1)^T$$:**
   $$F(3, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 9 + 1 - 5 \\ 3 - 1 - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad J_F(3, 1) = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}.$$
3. **Solve the linear system $$J_F(3,1)\,\Delta x = -F(3,1)$$:**
   - Determinant: $$\det(J_F) = 6(-1) - 2(1) = -8$$.
   - Inverse: $$J_F^{-1} = -\frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} -1 & -2 \\ -1 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & -6 \end{bmatrix}$$.
   - Step: $$\Delta x = -\frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & -6 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.875 \\ 0.125 \end{bmatrix}$$.
4. **Update:** $$x^{(1)} = (3, 1)^T + (-0.875, 0.125)^T = (2.125, 1.125)^T$$. Notice the second residual $$x_1 - x_2 - 1 = 2.125 - 1.125 - 1 = 0$$ is satisfied *exactly* in one step because it is linear, while the nonlinear error drops from $$5$$ to $$2.125^2 + 1.125^2 - 5 = 0.78125$$!

In this chapter, we apply Newton's method to the root finding problem. The Newton's method used in optimization problems has some differences, which are explained here. [[Newton's method in optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_method_in_optimization)][[Newton's method in root finding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_method)]

## Newton's method for root finding
Suppose we have a vector function $$F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{n}$$. The root finding problem is to find $$x$$ such that $$F(x) = 0$$.

>$$
>\begin{align}
>F(x) = 0.
>\end{align}
>$$

This problem is solved by choosing an initial value $$x^{(0)}$$ and applying Newton's method iteratively:

>$$
>\begin{align}
>&\text{choose initial }x^{(0)}\in \mathbb{R}^{n},\\\\
>&x^{(k)}=x^{(k-1)}-\nabla F(x^{(k-1)})^{-1}F(x^{(k-1)}), \qquad k=1,2,3,...\\\\
>\end{align}
>$$

Here, $$\nabla F(x^{(k-1)})$$ is the Jacobian matrix of $$F$$ at $$x^{(k-1)}$$. The Newton step $$x^{+}=x-\nabla F(x)^{-1}F(x)$$ can be derived using the linear approximation of $$F$$:

>$$
>\begin{align}
>F(y)\approx F(x) + F^{'}(x)(y-x) = 0\\\\
>y = x^{+}=x-F^{'}(x)^{-1}F(x).
>\end{align}
>$$

## Newton's method for optimization problem
Suppose we apply Newton's method to an optimization problem formulated as:

>$$
>\begin{align}
>\min_{x} F(x)
>\end{align}
>$$

This is equivalent to applying Newton's method to the root finding problem for the gradient $$\nabla{F(x)}=0$$ of the objective function $$F(x)$$.

In summary, unlike the problem of finding the root of a given function's derivative ($$\nabla F=0$$) using Newton's method in optimization problems, the root finding problem requires finding the root of the function value itself ($$F=0$$) using Newton's method. This results in a difference of one order in the derivative term in the update formula for $$x$$ in Newton's method for each problem.
