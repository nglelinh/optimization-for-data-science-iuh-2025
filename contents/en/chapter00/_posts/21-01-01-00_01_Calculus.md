Every iterative optimization algorithm is a disciplined way of taking steps in the dark using only local terrain measurements. Calculus provides the altimeter, the compass, and the curvature gauge: continuity ensures the terrain does not teleport beneath your feet, gradients tell you which way is downhill, and second derivatives tell you how steeply the ground bends so you do not overshoot the valley floor.

## Background Knowledge Map: How Calculus Powers Optimization

- **Continuity & Lipschitz Smoothness:** Guarantees that small parameter perturbations yield controlled changes in objective value and gradient, enabling step-size bounds that prevent divergence.
- **Derivatives & Gradients:** Characterize stationary points via $$\nabla f(x^*) = 0$$ and identify steepest descent directions $$-\nabla f(x)$$ in high-dimensional parameter spaces.
- **Directional Derivatives:** Project the gradient onto any candidate search direction $$d$$ via $$\nabla f(x)^T d$$, giving the exact algebraic test for a descent direction ($$\nabla f(x)^T d < 0$$).
- **Taylor Approximations:** Replace complex nonlinear functions locally with linear (first-order) or quadratic (second-order) surrogates—the direct foundation of gradient descent and Newton's method.

### Step-by-Step Worked Example (Read Slowly): One Calculus-Driven Step of Gradient Descent

Take the smooth scalar objective $$f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^2 - 3x + 5$$ starting at $$x_0 = 0$$ with step size $$\alpha = 0.5$$.

1. **Evaluate initial loss and slope:**
   $$f(0) = 5, \qquad f'(x) = x - 3 \implies f'(0) = -3.$$
   Because $$f'(0) < 0$$, moving in the positive direction decreases $$f$$.
2. **Apply the gradient descent update:**
   $$x_1 = x_0 - \alpha f'(x_0) = 0 - 0.5(-3) = 1.5.$$
3. **Measure the decrease:**
   $$f(1.5) = \frac{1}{2}(1.5)^2 - 3(1.5) + 5 = 1.125 - 4.5 + 5 = 1.625 < 5.$$
4. **Compare against the exact stationary point:** Setting $$f'(x^*) = x^* - 3 = 0$$ gives $$x^* = 3$$ with $$f''(3) = 1 > 0$$ and global minimum $$f(3) = 0.5$$. Notice that one step cut the distance to $$x^*$$ exactly in half ($$|3 - 1.5| = 1.5$$ vs. $$|3 - 0| = 3$$).

Optimization talks about *change*: a step that decreases a loss, a constraint that becomes tight, a Taylor model that is locally accurate. That language is calculus. This overview is the map for the four calculus lessons that follow; it does not replace them.

## What you must be fluent in

- **Continuity** so that a descent sequence cannot jump over a minimizer, and so that a continuous function on a compact set attains its min (Weierstrass).
- **Derivatives and the multivariable chain rule**, because every neural-net gradient is a long chain rule — a story we return to in [Chapter 27]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter27/).
- **Gradients and directional derivatives.** The first-order model $$f(x+d) \approx f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top d$$ is the reason gradient descent (Chapter 06) works, and $$-\nabla f(x)$$ is the locally steepest-descent direction in Euclidean geometry.
- **Taylor expansions.** Newton’s method (Chapter 14) is exactly the step that minimizes the local quadratic $$f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top d + \tfrac12 d^\top \nabla^2 f(x)\, d$$.

A function $$f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$$ is differentiable at $$x$$ if

$$
\lim_{\|h\|\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)-\nabla f(x)^\top h}{\|h\|}=0.
$$

If $$\nabla f$$ itself is Lipschitz, the descent lemma

$$
f(y) \le f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y-x) + \tfrac{L}{2}\|y-x\|_2^2
$$

gives the step-size restriction $$t \le 1/L$$ used throughout first-order theory.
