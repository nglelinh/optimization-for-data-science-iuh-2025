Optimization talks about *change*: a step that decreases a loss, a constraint that becomes tight, a Taylor model that is locally accurate. That language is calculus. This overview is the map for the four calculus lessons that follow; it does not replace them.

## What you must be fluent in

- **Continuity** so that a descent sequence cannot jump over a minimizer, and so that a continuous function on a compact set attains its min (Weierstrass).
- **Derivatives and the multivariable chain rule**, because every neural-net gradient is a long chain rule — a story we return to in [Chapter 27]({{ site.baseurl }}/contents/en/chapter27/).
- **Gradients and directional derivatives.** The first-order model $$f(x+d) \approx f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top d$$ is the reason gradient descent (Chapter 06) works, and $$-\nabla f(x)$$ is the locally steepest-descent direction in Euclidean geometry.
- **Taylor expansions.** Newton’s method (Chapter 14) is exactly the step that minimizes the local quadratic $$f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top d + \tfrac12 d^\top \nabla^2 f(x)\, d$$.

A function $$f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$$ is differentiable at $$x$$ if

$$
\lim_{\|h\|\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)-\nabla f(x)^\top h}{\|h\|}=0.
$$

If $$\nabla f$$ itself is Lipschitz, the descent lemma

$$
f(y) \le f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y-x) + \tfrac{L}{2}\|y-x\|_2^2
$$

gives the step-size restriction $$t \le 1/L$$ used throughout first-order theory.

## Lessons in this block

1. Continuity and uniform continuity
2. Derivatives and multivariable calculus
3. Gradient and directional derivatives
4. Taylor series

Read them in order, then try this check: write $$\nabla f$$ and $$\nabla^2 f$$ for $$f(x)=\tfrac12 x^\top Q x + q^\top x$$ with $$Q$$ symmetric. If that is automatic, you are ready for convex functions in Chapter 03.
